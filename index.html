Verbal — Synonyms

Choose the word closest in meaning to the word in capital letters. Answer as many as you like and check them; the rest stay open for later.

Where the misses are — and what to do about them
  1. The largest group is not a trap at all. In most of these the headword was understood and the answer word was the unknown one — CORROBORATE was clear, verify was not. When reviewing a card, learn the answer word as hard as the word in capitals; that is the half that keeps costing points.
  2. Say the word to yourself and ask which word it is not. counsel/council, ail/aid, condone/condemn, surly/surely, germane/German, inane/insane, rout/route. The look-alike is almost always sitting among the five choices, waiting.
  3. Do not trust an affix to carry a feeling. -ful is not always positive (BALEFUL), a- is not always negative (AFFABLE), and the list in LISTLESS is not a list. Split the word only when you know what the pieces actually mean: ef- + fuse, dis- + tend, e- + radix.
  4. An exact opposite feels related. When a choice sits on the same axis as the word, that is a sign you have found the right subject, not the right direction — check which end you are on before choosing.
  5. The effect of a thing is not its meaning. TRIUMPH is not happiness, INTRICATE is not confusing, MATURE is not capable. Ask what the word is, not what it leads to.

Verbal — Analogies

Read the first pair as a sentence, then pick the pair whose relationship matches. Answer as many as you like and check them; the rest stay open for later.

Where the misses are — and what to do about them
  1. Say the relationship in a full sentence first, before looking at the choices. Not “both are animals” but “a needle is the tool you sew with.” Then read each choice as the same sentence. A sentence that is too vague lets three choices through; add words until only one survives.
  2. Watch the direction on same-and-opposite pairs — this is the biggest group here. If the two words in the stem mean the same thing, the answer must be two words that mean the same thing. INFANTILE and PUERILE are synonyms, so miserly is to generous cannot be it; ENERVATE and ENERGIZE are opposites, so obstruct is to block cannot be it. A neat-looking pair of the wrong kind is the standard trap.
  3. Subject matter is bait. Chicken and pig are farm animals, but the question is the size gap, not the farm. Two words about the same topic as the stem are almost never the answer.
  4. If the stem is a person and a quality, decide whether the person has it or lacks it. An oaf lacks acumen; a scoundrel lacks virtue. Half the wrong choices pair a person with a quality they plainly have.
  5. When two choices both fit, the difference is precision. Go back and make the sentence stricter — who does it, to what, and how completely — rather than picking whichever feels better.

Reading

Only the questions that were missed on the test. Read one passage at a time, or open a whole session — or everything — to work through or print in one go. The passages are printed in full and the line numbers match the book, so questions that point at a line still work.

Where the misses are — and what to do about them
  1. Decide before you look. For a main idea, purpose, title or text-type question, say in one word what the writer is doing — explaining, praising, correcting, remembering — and only then read the choices. The wrong answers are real sentences from the passage; they are just not what the whole passage is for. On the gnu passage the first and last paragraphs both say the genes do the programming, and that repetition was the answer.
  2. Tone is the writer’s attitude, not the feelings in the story. The penny passage is full of a child’s entitlement, but the narrator looking back is nostalgic. The Pony Express writer sets legend beside fact to explain it, not because he disbelieves it.
  3. For a word in context, put each choice back in the sentence. The passage almost always supplies a contrast or a definition nearby: inherent sits opposite “may have to be learned,” and only in “stops only to eat” means just.
  4. For a literary device, check for like or as first. If the comparison is signalled, it is a simile; if it is stated flatly, a metaphor. If the phrase is one English already owns — rode for dear life — it is an idiom, not an invention of the writer.
  5. Inference means one small step, not a new idea. Find the sentence that carries the answer, then take the shortest step from it. If a choice needs two steps, it is the trap.
